* The caveats

* Polls are less reliable than they used to be, and nobody really knows how predictive prediction markets are.

Polls. Most people no longer answer calls from numbers they don't recognize, so pollsters now reach far fewer of the people they try, and they lean on text messages, online panels and statistical weighting to make the people they do reach look like the electorate. That works when the people who answer resemble the people who don't, and it can fail when they differ in ways the weighting misses. Every poll here also has a margin of error, and the New York City columns of statewide polls rest on smaller samples whose size the pollsters usually don't publish. Read one poll as one estimate, and read a gap of a few points as noise.

Prediction markets. A market price says what traders are willing to pay, not what the public thinks. Many New York markets trade little money, so a single trader can move the price. Manifold uses play money. There's no long record showing how well markets on state and city questions forecast outcomes, so treat a price as one more read, not a probability anyone has verified.

How this page works

What's in and what's out

Included: polls of all New York City or all New York State adults or voters, fielded in 2026, by pollsters that publish field dates, sample size and their questions. Every figure was read from the pollster's own release, toplines or crosstabs, and each row links to that document along with a passage copied from it.

Left out: polls of a single congressional or legislative district, campaign polls released without full questions and results, and any poll whose numbers could be found only in news coverage. Each one is listed with the reason in the list of polls left out, and the methodology explains the rules.

Reading the rows

  • New York City columns of statewide polls. Siena and others publish results for New York City respondents inside their statewide polls. These rows are marked "city column." The city sample is smaller than the statewide one and its size is usually not published, so the uncertainty is larger and unknown.
  • Who was asked. All adults, registered voters or likely voters, as the pollster describes the sample. Some questions were put to only part of the sample, such as Democrats in a primary question; the row says so.
  • Kinds of question. Job approval, favorability and an excellent-to-poor grade are different questions and are never combined. A favorable opinion of a person is not approval of the job they're doing.
  • Figures are shown exactly as the pollster published them. The page calculates nothing except where dots sit on the charts and, in the "where it ranks" lists, an issue's place among the answers to a most-important-issue question (how many answers drew a larger share, leaving out "other" and "don't know"). Results may not add to 100 because of rounding or answers the pollster didn't report.
